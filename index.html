Algorithmic Forensics

Prompt engineering strategy

We used AI as a supervised analyst. Prompts were directive and constraint-based. We required AI to work only from raw government files we downloaded. Every derived dataset was saved as JSON recording its source file. When outputs drifted, we corrected with explicit constraints.

Auditing AI data manipulation

Following our Chain of Custody Audit Card, we archived raw files unaltered with a manifest of their origins and checksums. Every transformation runs through a script that logs what it did: which row it read as the header, which columns it left as text, and which source file each year came from. Nothing is rounded or imputed. We audited outputs by comparing each new version of a graph against the previous one.

Mitigating bias and hallucination

Our audit caught one problem directly. AI swapped the Census’s April–June 2020 figure for a full-year number from an older release, and that changed the shape of the graph. We reverted it and now flag partial years instead of altering them. The AI also flagged claims the data didn’t support, such as “negative immigration in 2026” and “a 300% rise”.

Methodologies behind the visualized metrics

  • Migration: Census net international migration, years ending 1 July. Data for 2001–2009 come from the 2009 estimates release, 2010–2019 from the 2020 release and 2020–2025 from the 2025 release. On the county map, one dot = 100 people.
  • Visas: State Department visas issued in 2024, one dot = 50 visas. Arcs route through likely ports of entry; they illustrate, but do not record, journeys.
  • Green cards: DHS data for the 200 busiest counties.
  • Detention: ICE average daily detention by facility, plotted at ZIP code centres.
  • Removals: removals that began with an ICE arrest.

Limitations. Dots are placed approximately around ZIP code and county centres. Visa, green-card and ICE data run on fiscal years, while the Census counts years ending 1 July.
